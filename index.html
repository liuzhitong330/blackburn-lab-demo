Your lab connects telomere maintenance to human health through clinical studies and careful molecular measurements. I was drawn to the practical question underneath a longitudinal result: did the biological sample change, or did the conditions under which we measured it change?

I built a specimen-history audit of the public CRELES data, whose telomere assays were performed in the Blackburn laboratory. 159 of 967 paired changes switch direction under the paper's adjustment. That does not tell us which representation is true. It tells us why the sample record belongs beside the result.

How it works

Before interpreting telomere change, inspect storage histories and then compare the same person's change in deposited T/S with values reconstructed from the paper's normalization equations. T/S is a relative qPCR ratio, not base pairs. This is an unweighted descriptive audit, not a new causal analysis or a biological-age score.

Matching three categories does not match storage duration, cell composition or every pre-analytical condition. A sign switch does not prove an assay error. These are unweighted descriptive summaries, not a re-estimation of the paper's inferential models.

Why this matters for the lab

The next step I would discuss is a balanced bridge-aliquot study: retain matched aliquots from an earlier collection and re-assay them alongside the next wave, with shared controls. Record extraction, storage, transport and assay history separately. This can help distinguish run-to-run shifts from a change in the specimen; the present observational audit cannot separate them causally.

A reusable next-wave record

For a future PBMC study, extend the ledger to participant and visit keys, draw and processing timestamps, collection tube, viable cell count, aliquot ID, transfer temperatures/times, extraction method, storage/freeze–thaw history, assay lot, control identity and repeat measurements. Preserve missing values and record deviations for review; do not invent acceptance limits.

Compare repeated measurements from the same aliquot and independent preparations. A shared-control shift would motivate run review; a preparation-dependent shift would motivate a handling investigation. Interpretation still needs the assay team's validation and the study statistician.

Methods note

Processed-data reanalysis of Rosero-Bixby et al., PLOS ONE (2019), using its S1 data and S2 normalization description. The source is a population cohort of older Costa Rican adults with mixed-leukocyte DNA—not the lab's current PBMC study. No clinical outcome, raw qPCR well data or telomerase readout is inferred here.

Analysis choices, uncertainty and reproducibility

The complete analysis record accompanies the downloadable reproduction script.

The source normalization extrapolates to a reference condition without direct support in every assay lot. It is shown to expose assumption-dependence, not to recover a true biological value. Different observed changes can also reflect biological variation or unmeasured conditions.
